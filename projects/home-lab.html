Home lab: teaching a low-cost arm real tasks.

A running set of experiments with cheap arms on my living-room table, pointed at ordinary household jobs. The interesting part is not that the policies work, it is discovering exactly what they have and have not learned when they do.

Status Ongoing
Hardware Low-cost arms · XLeRobot
Policies ACT · SmolVLA · Diffusion · MolmoAct · π0.5
Stack LeRobot · Hugging Face

The setup

Everything here runs on inexpensive arms driven by the LeRobot stack: teleoperate the arm to record demonstrations, push the dataset to Hugging Face, train a policy on it, then run that policy back on the same hardware. No motion planner, no hand-written controller. The robot learns the task from the demonstrations or it does not do the task.

That makes the failure modes unusually informative, which is really why this page exists. When a classical pipeline fails you can trace it to a line of code. When a learned policy fails, you have to design an experiment to find out what it actually learned.

Weekend hackathon: toasting bread

The starting point was a hackathon: a four-person team, two days, one task. Put a slice of bread into a toaster, press the button, and take the bread back out once it pops.

It sounds trivial and is not. It is three separate manipulation problems chained together, each with a different failure mode, and a mistake in any one of them ends the run. Picking up a floppy slice of bread is a deformable-object grasp. Pressing the lever needs force in a specific direction against a compliant mechanism. Retrieving the toast means reaching into a narrow slot. We trained ACT and SmolVLA policies on teleoperated demonstrations to get through it.

Library robot: picking and stacking books

After the hackathon we bought the robot, and the team started building library applications on it: picking up books and stacking them. Books are a good next step up from bread. They are rigid, which removes the deformable-grasp problem, but they vary hugely in size, thickness, cover design and how they sit on a surface.

The encouraging result here is that a plain ACT policy generalized further than expected. It kept working when handed a book it had not been trained on, and it kept working when the book was partially occluded, which is the case that usually breaks a policy that has simply memorized a visual template.

Desk cleanup: the pen task

This is the experiment I have pushed hardest on, and the one that has taught me the most, largely by not working.

First attempt: more data, three policies, poor results

I collected two datasets, both teleoperated on the same table:

  • 40 episodes with a single pen dropped into a bin, varying the pen's location.
  • 60 episodes with multiple pens on the table, still picking up only one of them.

I merged the two and trained three policies on the combined set: SmolVLA, a diffusion policy, and π0.5.

The results were poor across the board. None of the three performed well. My working hypothesis is that the merge itself was the problem: mixing single-pen and multi-pen scenes gives the policy two visually different situations that demand the same behavior, and with this little data that ambiguity is more likely to confuse it than to teach it robustness. More data would probably help; a cleaner task definition seemed more likely to.

I also could not properly evaluate π0.5, for an unglamorous reason: my GPU has 8 GB of VRAM and the model is too large to comfortably run inference on, never mind train.

Simplify: one orange pen

So I cut the task down to its simplest honest version: a single orange pen, 40 recorded episodes of picking it up and dropping it into a pouch. Get that solid, then add variation back one axis at a time.

What ACT actually learned

It worked. Then probing it revealed three quite specific limits, and together they say something about what the policy had really picked up:

  • Color is doing more work than it should. The policy handled the orange pen it trained on, and also a purple one, but failed on green, blue and brown pens. Same shape, same task, same location. The visual feature it locked onto does not transfer across color.
  • It memorized the drop location rather than the target. Take the pouch away entirely and the robot still travels to where the pouch used to be and releases the pen over empty table. It never learned "put it in the pouch", it learned "go here and open the gripper".
  • Orientation is brittle. Significant changes to the pen's orientation cause failures, even in positions it otherwise handles.

MolmoAct: swapping in a VLM backbone

The color result was the interesting thread to pull. If the policy fails on a green pen purely because it has never seen green, then a model carrying real visual-semantic priors should not have that problem, since it already knows what a pen is independently of this dataset.

So I trained MolmoAct v2, which has a VLM backbone, on the exact same dataset. No new demonstrations, no relabeling, just a different model over identical data. It handled green, blue and the other pens quite well, which is a clean result: the limitation was in the representation, not in the data.

The best run so far came out of this. With two pens placed on the table, the robot picked them up one at a time and placed each in the bin.

What I take from it so far

  • A working demo tells you very little. ACT picking up the orange pen looks identical to ACT understanding the task. The difference only shows up when you deliberately break an assumption, which is why removing the pouch was the most useful five minutes of the project.
  • More data was the wrong instinct. My first move on poor results was to merge datasets and train bigger models. Cutting the task down to one pen taught me considerably more than the merged set did.
  • Backbone priors buy generalization that data collection would have to pay for. Same 40 episodes, same task; the VLM-backed policy generalized across colors where ACT could not. Collecting demonstrations in every pen color would have worked too, and would have cost far more.
  • Hardware sets the ceiling. 8 GB of VRAM quietly rules out an entire class of model, which is a real constraint on what a home lab can evaluate rather than a footnote.

This is all live and ongoing. Next is adding variation back onto the single-pen task in a controlled way, orientation and location before color, and then moving to multiple pens once the simple case is genuinely solid rather than apparently solid.
