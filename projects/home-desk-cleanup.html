Desk cleanup: the pen task.

Pick a pen off the desk and drop it in the bin. This is the experiment I have pushed hardest on and learned the most from, largely by not working. The most useful result was discovering that a policy which looked like it had solved the task had done nothing of the kind.

First attempt: more data, three policies, poor results

I collected two datasets, both teleoperated on the same table:

  • 40 episodes with a single pen dropped into a bin, varying the pen's location.
  • 60 episodes with multiple pens on the table, still picking up only one of them.

I merged the two and trained three policies on the combined set: SmolVLA, a diffusion policy, and π0.5.

The results were poor across the board. None of the three performed well. My working hypothesis is that the merge itself was the problem: mixing single-pen and multi-pen scenes gives the policy two visually different situations that demand the same behavior, and with this little data that ambiguity is more likely to confuse it than to teach it robustness. More data would probably help; a cleaner task definition seemed more likely to.

I also could not properly evaluate π0.5, for an unglamorous reason: my GPU has 8 GB of VRAM and the model is too large to comfortably run inference on, never mind train. π0.5 is a 3.3B-parameter model, and reported inference for its π0 predecessor sits around 13.6 GB of VRAM, so this is a hard wall rather than a tuning problem. Worth stating plainly, because it rules out an entire class of model from a home setup.

Simplify: one orange pen

So I cut the task down to its simplest honest version: a single orange pen, 40 recorded episodes of picking it up and dropping it into a pouch. Get that solid, then add variation back one axis at a time.

And it worked. Which is where it gets interesting, because a policy that works is the hardest kind to learn anything from until you start deliberately breaking assumptions.

The factor sweep

With a working baseline, the useful move is to change exactly one thing at a time and see what survives. The baseline is the trained condition: a single orange pen, in a trained orientation, dropped into the pouch it was demonstrated with.

Both policies below were trained on the same 40 episodes. Nothing about the data differs between the two columns, only the model.

What the sweep says

Three things fall out of that table, and together they are fairly damning about what ACT had really picked up.

  • Color is doing more work than it should. Orange and purple pass, green and blue and brown fail. Same shape, same task, same location. The visual feature the policy locked onto does not transfer across color, which means it is keying on appearance rather than on the object.
  • It memorized the drop location, not the target. Remove the pouch entirely and the robot still travels to where the pouch used to be and releases the pen over empty table. It never learned "put it in the pouch". It learned "go here and open the gripper".
  • Orientation is brittle. Significant rotation of the pen causes failures, even in positions the policy otherwise handles.

The pouch row is the one I keep coming back to. The policy was confidently wrong, and nothing inside it could detect that. It has no representation of the goal, only of the motion that usually satisfies the goal.

The gaps in the right-hand column are the honest state of things: MolmoAct has been swept across color and object count, not yet across the drop target or orientation. Those are the next runs, and the pouch-removal test in particular is the one I most want its answer to.

This is a known failure mode, which is reassuring

I went looking afterwards for whether any of this was documented, partly to check I had not simply trained something badly. It is, and both results have names.

The color result is shortcut learning: a policy relying on correlations between actions and task-irrelevant parts of the observation instead of the causal structure of the task. What surprised me is how specific the published finding is. Work analyzing factor bias in robot policies reports that color is the dominant factor policies over-rely on, which is exactly the axis my sweep broke on. That my orange policy transferred to purple but not to green is a small local instance of a general result.

The pouch result is causal confusion: the policy learned a correlate of the goal (the gripper opens at these coordinates) rather than the goal itself (the pen ends up in the container), and cannot tell the two apart because in every training episode they coincided. Nothing in 40 demonstrations distinguishes them, so there is nothing for the policy to be wrong about until the pouch moves.

The literature also suggests the fix I stumbled into is one of the recognized ones: reallocating a fixed demonstration budget toward under-grounded factors, rather than simply collecting more of the same. Which is a more precise version of what the merged-dataset run taught me the hard way.

MolmoAct: swapping in a VLM backbone

The color result was the interesting thread to pull. If the policy fails on a green pen purely because it has never seen green, then a model carrying real visual-semantic priors should not have that problem, since it already knows what a pen is independently of this dataset.

So I trained MolmoAct 2 on the exact same dataset. It is Ai2's action-reasoning model, built on the Molmo2-ER embodied-reasoning vision-language backbone with an action expert on top, so it arrives already carrying visual-semantic priors that a policy trained only on my 40 episodes cannot have. No new demonstrations, no relabeling, just a different model over identical data. It handled green, blue and the other pens quite well, which makes this a clean controlled result: the limitation was in the representation, not in the data.

The best run so far came out of this. With two pens placed on the table, the robot picked them up one at a time and placed each in the bin.

Where it goes next

Adding variation back onto the single-pen task in a controlled way, orientation and location before color, and then moving to multiple pens once the simple case is genuinely solid rather than apparently solid. The distinction between those two is the whole lesson of this experiment.
